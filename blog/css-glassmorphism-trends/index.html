Glassmorphism CSS: Design and Accessibility Guide

Published: May 13, 2026 Author: ASH, Founder of OmniTools Reading Time: 5 min

Glassmorphism — the frosted-glass interface style popularised by macOS Big Sur and Microsoft's Fluent Design — remains one of the most requested UI effects in modern web design. But behind its beauty hides a trap: poor contrast, sluggish performance and broken accessibility. This guide shows you how to build glassmorphism that is gorgeous and responsible.

1. What Exactly Is Glassmorphism?

Glassmorphism simulates a pane of frosted glass floating above a colourful background. Elements behind the "glass" appear blurred and tinted, while the panel itself stays crisp. The effect relies on the CSS backdrop-filter: blur() property combined with semi-transparent backgrounds, subtle borders and soft shadows. Done well, it creates depth and hierarchy without heavy imagery; done badly, it becomes an unreadable, battery-draining mess.

2. The Four Ingredients of the Frosted-Glass Look

Every convincing glass effect combines four layers, and missing any one of them breaks the illusion:

  • Transparency: A background like rgba(255, 255, 255, 0.08) lets the content behind bleed through. On dark themes use white at 5–15% opacity; on light themes, white at 40–60%.
  • Backdrop blur: backdrop-filter: blur(12px) saturate(150%) frosts whatever sits behind the panel. The saturate boost keeps colours vivid after blurring.
  • Hairline border: A 1px border at rgba(255, 255, 255, 0.18) simulates light catching the glass edge. Without it, panels look like fog rather than glass.
  • Soft shadow: A diffused shadow such as 0 8px 32px rgba(0, 0, 0, 0.37) lifts the panel off the background and sells the floating effect. You can fine-tune it visually with our CSS box shadow generator.
"Glassmorphism is not a blur filter. It is the careful layering of transparency, blur, edge light and shadow — remove one and the illusion collapses."

3. Accessibility: Where Glassmorphism Goes Wrong

The biggest criticism of glassmorphism is text legibility. Content behind the glass constantly shifts as users scroll, which means text contrast is unpredictable. WCAG 2.1 requires a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for body text — and a busy gradient behind frosted glass can silently destroy that. The fix is discipline: raise the panel opacity until text passes, add a subtle solid tint behind text blocks, and always test your palette with a contrast checker like the one built into our colour palette generator.

Also remember that backdrop-filter still lacks support in some older browsers. Always provide a fallback: wrap the effect in @supports (backdrop-filter: blur(1px)) and give non-supporting browsers a nearly opaque solid background. Users should get a slightly flatter design — never broken, invisible text.

4. Performance: The Hidden Cost of Blur

Backdrop blur forces the browser to re-render everything behind the panel on every scroll frame, and stacking multiple blurred layers multiplies the cost. On low-end mobiles this causes visible jank. Keep blur radii between 8px and 20px, limit glass panels to one or two per viewport, and never animate the blur value itself. Promote panels to their own compositor layer with will-change: transform where needed, and prefer transforming the panel over re-blurring the backdrop.

5. Common Glassmorphism Mistakes to Avoid

Even experienced designers fall into the same traps. The first is glass on glass — nesting a blurred panel inside another blurred panel, which muddies both and doubles the rendering cost. The second is using glass over plain solid backgrounds, where the blur has nothing to filter and the panel just looks washed out; glassmorphism needs colourful, textured backdrops to shine. The third is forgetting the saturate() boost, leaving panels looking grey and dusty. Finally, never place critical interactive elements — payment buttons, form inputs — on heavily blurred surfaces where focus states become hard to see. Save the frosted treatment for cards, navigation shells and decorative overlays.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Does backdrop-filter work in all browsers?

It works in all modern browsers (Chrome, Edge, Safari, Firefox 103+). For older browsers, wrap it in an @supports query and provide a more opaque solid background as a fallback.

Q: What blur value is best for glassmorphism?

Between 8px and 20px suits most interfaces. Lower values look like plain transparency; higher values wash out the background and hurt performance on mobile devices.

Q: How do I keep glassmorphism text readable?

Increase panel background opacity until body text reaches at least a 4.5:1 contrast ratio, add subtle solid tints behind text, and test against the busiest background the panel may cover.

Q: Is glassmorphism bad for performance?

Only when overused. Each blurred panel forces re-compositing of everything behind it. One or two glass panels per viewport with moderate blur is fine; entire blurred layouts will stutter on low-end phones.
