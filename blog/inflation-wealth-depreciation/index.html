How Inflation Affects Purchasing Power

Published: June 10, 2026 Reading Time: 5 min

Inflation is the quietest tax you will ever pay. No bill arrives, no statement itemises it — yet every year the same money buys a little less. Understanding how inflation erodes purchasing power is the foundation of every sensible savings, salary and retirement decision. This guide explains the mechanics in plain English and shows you how to run the numbers yourself.

1. What Inflation Actually Measures

Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices rises over time, usually tracked through indices like the UK's CPI (Consumer Prices Index), which prices a representative "basket" of hundreds of goods and services — food, energy, rent, transport. When CPI reads 3%, that basket costs 3% more than a year ago, which is the same as saying each pound buys about 3% less. Your personal inflation rate may differ from the headline figure depending on what you actually spend money on, but the direction is universal.

2. The Compounding Thief: Why Small Rates Become Big Losses

Inflation compounds exactly like investment growth — except against you. At a steady 3%, prices double roughly every 24 years; at 6%, every 12. That means £10,000 kept as cash for a decade at 3% inflation still says £10,000, but buys what £7,440 bought when you set it aside. This is why "money under the mattress" is not safety but a slow, guaranteed leak. You can see the effect on any amount with our free inflation calculator.

"Cash feels safe because its number never falls. Inflation ensures its value does. The riskiest long-term strategy is often the one that feels safest."

3. Nominal vs Real: The Only Distinction That Matters

Every financial number comes in two flavours. Nominal is the figure printed on the statement — your salary, your savings balance, your projected pension. Real is that figure adjusted for inflation — what it actually purchases. A 4% pay rise during 5% inflation is, in real terms, a pay cut. A savings account paying 2% during 3% inflation guarantees a 1% annual loss of purchasing power. Train yourself to translate every money question into real terms and half of financial marketing stops working on you.

4. How Different Assets Weather Inflation

  • Cash: The worst long-term defender. Interest rarely keeps pace with inflation after tax, so real value decays year after year.
  • Bonds: Fixed payments become less valuable as prices rise; inflation-linked gilts are the designed exception.
  • Equities: Historically the strongest long-run hedge, because companies can raise prices and grow earnings alongside the economy — though with real volatility along the way.
  • Property & commodities: Tangible assets tend to reprice with inflation, though local markets and costs complicate the story.

The pattern: assets tied to real economic activity defend purchasing power; assets promising fixed nominal returns quietly surrender it.

5. Defending Your Money: A Practical Checklist

First, hold only the cash you need — an emergency fund of three to six months' expenses — and invest the long-term surplus so growth can outrun inflation (see the maths in our guide to compound growth and financial independence). Second, negotiate salary in real terms: know the current inflation rate before accepting any percentage. Third, stress-test retirement plans at several inflation rates, because 2% and 4% worlds produce wildly different outcomes over 30 years. Each scenario takes seconds in the inflation calculator.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is a normal inflation rate?

Most central banks, including the Bank of England, target around 2% a year — enough to keep the economy flexible without eroding savings too fast. Rates have swung far higher in recent years, which is why personal inflation awareness matters.

Q: How do I calculate what my money will be worth in the future?

Divide the amount by (1 + inflation rate) raised to the number of years. £10,000 at 3% for 10 years: 10,000 / 1.03^10 ≈ £7,440 of today's purchasing power. An inflation calculator does this instantly.

Q: Is keeping money in a savings account enough to beat inflation?

Usually only when interest rates exceed inflation after tax. In low-rate or high-inflation periods, standard accounts lose real value, which is why long-term money typically needs growth assets.

Q: Can inflation ever be good for me?

Yes — for fixed-rate borrowers. Inflation shrinks the real value of a fixed mortgage or loan over time, so you repay with ever-cheaper pounds while wages and prices rise around it.
